What Happens During The 36-Hour Sprint

A Visual Tour of the Hackathon Lifecycle

  • Phase 2: The Midnight Grind
  • Phase 3: The Final Countdown

Introduction

Spring 2023. I remember the crushing weight of my backpack as I walked into the student center for my very first hackathon. I was a freshman, I didn't have a team, and the only programming language I knew was some Python. Around me, hundreds of students were already aggressively claiming tables, plugging in chargers, and unboxing hardware like monitors that they had somehow dragged onto a commuter train. It was definitely quite a lot to take in at once. I had no clue what to get, and how to navigate the arena.

That anxiety is exactly why HaxScore exists today. While the rest of this website catalogs the hardware you need and the venues you will visit, those reviews don't capture the actual human experience of surviving a 36-hour coding sprint. The purpose of this visual essay is to peel back the curtain and show you exactly what happens between the opening ceremony and the final judging. Whether you are a first-time attendee or a seasoned veteran, this is a chronological tour of the chaos, the camaraderie, and the caffeine that defines a hackathon.

Phase 1: Settling In and Ideating (Hours 1-12)

The first few hours of any hackathon are deceptively relaxed. We grab some merch and freebies, along with access codes for software offered by the organizers (usually MLH - Major League Hacking). Following the opening ceremonies (which usually involve a barrage of sponsor pitches and API demonstrations), the floor opens up for team formation. For solo attendees, this is a massive networking mixer. You pitch your half-baked ideas to strangers, hoping to find fellow hackers to join you, complement your backend skills, or provide feedback.

Once the teams are locked in, the whiteboards come out. This is the ideation phase. Nobody is writing code yet; everyone is drawing flowcharts, debating database architecture, and arguing over which JavaScript framework to use. It is a period of pure, unadulterated optimism. Your team genuinely believes you are going to build the next Facebook before Sunday morning.

Phase 2: The Midnight Grind (Hours 13-24)

By 1:00 AM, the optimism usually evaporates and is replaced by syntax errors and desperate Google searches. This is the heart of the project: The Midnight Grind. The ambient noise of the venue shifts from lively chatter to the furious, synchronized clacking of keyboards.

Pressure builds up and emotions flare. There's a midnight surprise with a prize. Everyone is gunning for it. It is a funny mix of cold pizza, energy drinks, and pure adrenaline. You will write some of the worst, most unscalable "spaghetti code" of your life during these hours, but as long as it compiles, nobody cares.

Eventually, human bodies give into the toll. By 4:00 AM, the hacking floor looks like a tech-themed triage center. Students are passed out in beanbag chairs, slumped over keyboards, or curled up in sleeping bags underneath their workstations. The smart ones take a strategic 3-hour nap. The brave ones push through the sunrise. Having done both, I would advise the former.

Phase 3: The Final Countdown (Hours 25-36)

The final phase is a blur of panic. The submission deadline is usually set for Sunday morning at 10:00 AM. At 9:30 AM, you realize your deployment server is crashing. The UI that looked beautiful on your teammate's Mac is completely broken on your Windows machine. It is a mad dash to hard-code variables, hide broken features, and record a demo video before the Devpost portal locks you out.

Once the submission goes through, a strange sense of euphoria washes over the room. You transition into the science-fair portion of the event, standing by your table and pitching your duct-taped project to industry judges. You are exhausted, you smell terrible, and your throat hurts from talking, but you are immensely proud of what you built from scratch in a day and a half. The judges ask you questions about scalability and future plans, but you have no idea how to answer. You just hope they are impressed by the fact that it works at all.

Conclusion: The Value of the Sprint

Looking back at that hackathon, I ended up joining a team of three upperclassmen who needed someone to write basic Python and SQL queries. We didn't win anything. Our project barely functioned. But sitting with them at 1:00 PM after the final ceremony, debugging errors and sharing a box of stale donuts, I realized I had learned more about real-world software development in 36 hours than I had in my entire previous semester of lectures.

If you take away anything from this visual essay, let it be this: hackathons are not really just about the code. They are about resilience, rapid problem-solving, and the shared trauma of building something impossible under an absurd deadline. As HaxScore continues to grow, we'll be covering larger, 48-hour global events, but the underlying heartbeat of the event will always remain exactly the same. Just remember to pack a sleeping bag and eye mask along with noise canceling headphones. Trust Me, You Will Need Them!!
