About the Project

I have always aspired to reduce the waste I generate. I could talk about sustainability in abstract terms, but to be honest, my habits have been invisible to me from the moment the bag left my hand. As a result, I have started tracking my trash each week to understand my usage and consumption patterns. Writing it down forces me to confront my reliance on packaged food, the amount of plastic said convenience generates, and how quickly those "small" items add up. I hope sharing this personal effort encourages you to see your habits in a new light and feel inspired to start your own journey.

Beyond the confines of my household, I believe that making this data public can spark a necessary conversation about systemic waste. It is one thing to know that society has a trash problem, and yet another to see a week's worth of non-recyclable debris laid out as a personal footprint. Sharing this journey builds up the path towards better personal accountability. When habits are visible, even in a small way, they feel more real and harder to ignore. It encourages others to reflect on their own waste and consider collective actions that can lead to systemic change.

Over the duration of this exercise, it became less about guilt and more about awareness. I have started pausing before purchases and asking whether something is necessary, reusable, or avoidable. Documenting my trash each week turns an automatic routine into a conscious practice, helping me align my actions more closely with the values I already claim to hold. This shift from guilt to mindful action can inspire others to see waste tracking as a tool for positive change rather than self-criticism.

This practice effectively challenges the myth that throwing something "away" makes it disappear. As archaeologist Sarah Newman states, "There is not, nor has there ever been, an 'away' for things to go". Disposal is ultimately a spatial illusion as (waste) matter continues to exist even when it leaves our sight (as we learnt in Physics: no matter can be destroyed). By visually cataloging these items and their destinations, this project aims to provide a level of "explicit intent" that is often missing from the ancient refuse heaps Newman explores. While archaeologists must often guess whether a discovered object was a ritual deposit or merely unwanted, documenting my own trash captures the exact moment an object transitions from "valuable" to "trash" in my mind. It validates the claim that waste is a decision, not a physical state.

However, viewing trash as a natural fact of life is not feasible. As Rozina Khattak notes, "global municipal solid waste production [of over 11 billion tonnes] [...] is enough to fill over 800,000 Olympic-sized swimming pools" annually. Much of this garbage is burned in furnaces, producing toxic greenhouse gases that puncture our precious ozone layer (which protects us from the rays of the Sun) and create a public health crisis (often increased rates of cancer) in vulnerable communities across the globe. Recognizing the scale of this issue can evoke a sense of shared responsibility and motivate us to consider how our individual choices contribute to systemic waste problems.

In such circumstances, we should pause and take a look at the work of Vik Muniz, who transforms the worst material imaginable to examine the "place of desire […] the narrow gap between wanting and doing, mind and matter". My weekly tracking is a map of that very gap, i.e. the physical residue of convenience and impulse. Confronting this unvarnished truth is essential because our current landfill crisis is driven by the sheer volume of matter outstripping the planet's capacity to contain it. By elevating these discarded materials from invisible burdens to objects of study, this project aims to bridge the distance between my momentary decisions and the systemic shifts required for a circular economy. Making these habits visible ensures they are no longer an inevitable fact of life, but a series of choices that can be reimagined.

In the end, this project inspires others to reflect on their own waste habits and consider small changes that can collectively make a big difference. By sharing my journey, I want to contribute to a broader conversation about sustainability and encourage others to take steps to reduce their environmental impact. Your stories and efforts can empower others to see that change is possible and within reach.

About Me

Hi, I'm Sumedh Sinha!

I am a senior majoring in Computer Science at Rutgers University - New Brunswick.

I'm originally from New Delhi, India, and first came to the US in 2022 to begin my undergraduate studies. I expect to graduate in May 2026 (i.e. this spring!), and work as a full time software developer.

I possess strong analytical and problem solving skills that I have gained with my coursework and experiences over the years. I am proficient in Python, SQL, Java, and C. Combined with a keen sense of observation, and ability to think on my feet, I aspire for perfection on tight deadlines. I've also taken part in a couple of hackathons over the past few years, and have led many diverse teams during that time. My team and I won a $750 award for our project during the Fall 2024 Rutgers HealthHack.

I'm currently working on-campus as a Teaching Assistant (TA) for a foundational computer science course and a Research Assistant (RA) for WINLAB (Wireless Information Network Laboratory) at Rutgers. As a TA, I work with over 25 students in my section leading weekly sessions that review course concepts, assignments and quizzes. I provide support during office hours, assist the course coordinator with logistics, and maintain academic integrity throughout the semester.

I love reading about current events, traveling, and watching thriller movies/series (lately been watching House, M.D. and The Expanse).
